Why We Plan the Menu After the Market, Not Before

Most restaurants plan a menu and then buy ingredients to fill it. We do the opposite, and the difference shows up directly in the quality of what actually reaches the table.

A fixed menu locks in a decision before you know the facts

Committing to a specific dish weeks in advance means committing to buying whatever ingredient that dish needs, regardless of whether it's actually at its best that week. Planning after seeing the market removes this guesswork entirely — the menu reflects what's genuinely good that day, not what was assumed good when the menu was printed.

Quality varies more than most diners realize

The same type of fish or vegetable can vary meaningfully in quality week to week, depending on weather, season, and simple availability. A kitchen locked into a fixed menu has to work around whatever quality shows up, while a market-first approach can lean into whatever happens to be exceptional that particular morning.

This approach requires real flexibility in the kitchen

Cooking this way means the kitchen team needs to be comfortable building a full menu with only a few hours of lead time, rather than following a script prepared weeks in advance. This takes real skill and confidence, but it produces food that tastes more alive than a menu executed the same way regardless of what's actually available.

Guests get told the menu later than they might expect

Because the menu isn't finalized until the market run is done, guests reserving a seat are trusting the kitchen with the specifics rather than choosing from a a la carte list in advance. This is a genuine trade-off, but it's also exactly what lets each evening reflect real, current ingredients rather than a locked-in plan.

Waste goes down when buying comes first

Buying specifically for a predetermined menu often means over-ordering to guarantee availability, which increases waste when quality or quantity doesn't match expectations. Buying first and planning around it lets us purchase exactly what looks right that day, reducing the guesswork that leads to excess.

Relationships with suppliers become genuinely important

This approach only works well with suppliers who are honest about quality and willing to flag what's actually excellent that day, rather than simply fulfilling a standing order. Building those relationships over time is part of what makes market-first planning consistently reliable rather than a gamble each week.

Consistency comes from process, not from a fixed dish

Some worry that changing the menu constantly sacrifices consistency, but we think about consistency differently — as a reliable process and standard of care applied to whatever ingredient shows up, rather than an identical dish reproduced regardless of ingredient quality.

This method doesn't scale the way a fixed menu does

Market-first planning is genuinely harder to scale to a large operation serving many covers a night, since it depends on a kitchen team with real judgment and flexibility rather than a repeatable, standardized process. This is part of why we've deliberately kept seatings small rather than trying to grow this approach beyond what it can actually support well.

The takeaway

Planning a menu after seeing the market, rather than before, means every dish reflects what was actually good that day rather than a decision made weeks earlier. It requires more flexibility and trust from both the kitchen and the guest, but it's what lets each evening's menu genuinely earn its place on the table.
