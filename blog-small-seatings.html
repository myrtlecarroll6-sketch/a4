Why We Keep Every Seating Small

It would be easy to seat more people each evening, and it would likely be more profitable in the short term. We've deliberately chosen not to, because a larger table changes far more than just the headcount.

Attention per plate drops as covers increase

A kitchen built to plate a small number of covers can give real, individual attention to each plate leaving the pass. Scale that same kitchen up significantly without changing its structure, and that attention gets spread thinner, even if the food is technically still "the same" dish.

Timing gets harder to control at scale

A small seating lets a kitchen time courses precisely, sending each one out at the right moment for that specific plate. A larger seating introduces more variables — more orders in flight, more room for a course to sit too long before it's served — that a market-first, flexible menu approach handles less predictably at higher volume.

The room itself changes character with size

A small table creates a genuinely different social atmosphere than a large dining room split across many separate tables. Conversation flows differently, and guests often end up talking across what would otherwise be strangers, which is a real part of the experience we're trying to create, not an incidental side effect.

Market-first planning has a natural ceiling

Buying based on the morning's market, rather than a standing wholesale order, only works at a certain scale — beyond that, you're forced back into predictable, advance ordering that undermines the whole approach. Keeping seatings small keeps this market-first method genuinely workable rather than diluted into something that only resembles it.

Consistency is easier to maintain with fewer variables

Every additional guest adds complexity — dietary considerations, timing coordination, portion consistency — that compounds as numbers grow. A smaller seating keeps this complexity manageable enough that quality doesn't quietly slip as a trade-off for higher volume.

Scarcity is a genuine part of the experience, not just marketing

Limited seats mean a seat at the table means something specific — reserving ahead, planning around a date. This isn't purely a scarcity marketing tactic; it's a direct consequence of choosing to prioritize the quality of a small evening over the volume of a larger one.

Staff can focus on hospitality, not just throughput

A small seating lets service staff engage genuinely with guests — explaining a dish, answering a real question — rather than moving quickly through a larger room purely to keep pace with volume. This changes the entire feel of the evening for guests as much as the food does.

Growth, if it happens, should preserve this core structure

If we ever expand, it would likely mean adding more seatings on more evenings, each still kept small, rather than making any single evening larger. The small-table structure is core to what this format actually is, not simply a limitation to outgrow.

The takeaway

Keeping every seating small is a deliberate trade-off, not a limitation we're working around — it protects the market-first menu planning, the kitchen's ability to give real attention to each plate, and the communal atmosphere that a fixed, single-menu evening is built around.
