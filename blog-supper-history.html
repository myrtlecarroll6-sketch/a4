HISTORY

A Short History of the Supper Club

The modern supper club format, with its fixed menu and communal table, has roots in much older, more informal dining traditions that predate the term itself by a considerable stretch.

Early supper clubs began as informal social gatherings

Long before the format was commercialized, informal supper gatherings among friends or community groups served primarily a social function, with the meal itself often being a secondary consideration to the gathering and conversation it supported.

Home dinner parties provided an early structural model

The tradition of hosting a fixed, unannounced menu for guests at a home dinner party — where guests eat what's served rather than choosing from options — closely mirrors the fixed-menu supper club format, suggesting a direct lineage from private hospitality traditions into a more public, bookable version.

Speakeasy-era supper clubs added a commercial, communal dimension

During a period of history when certain venues operated with a degree of informality or discretion, supper clubs combining food, atmosphere, and social gathering became a recognizable commercial format, often featuring a set menu and a communal or semi-communal dining structure.

Post-war supper clubs became associated with a specific aesthetic

In a later era, supper clubs became strongly associated with a particular polished, formal aesthetic — a set menu, a specific style of service — that shaped popular associations with the term for decades afterward, even as the format itself continued evolving.

Underground and pop-up dinners revived the format more recently

A more recent wave of informal, sometimes secretive pop-up dinners hosted in unconventional spaces reintroduced a fixed-menu, communal dining format to a new generation, often explicitly drawing on the older supper club tradition while adapting it to smaller, more experimental settings.

Chef-led tasting formats merged with the supper club structure

As chef-driven tasting menu formats grew in popularity in fine dining more broadly, some of that approach — a single, chef-determined sequence of courses — merged with the communal supper club structure, producing something closer to today's format: one fixed menu, one shared table, decided by the kitchen rather than the guest.

Market-driven menu planning reflects a broader culinary shift

The specific practice of building a menu around market availability rather than a fixed plan reflects a broader shift in culinary culture toward valuing ingredient quality and seasonality, which found a natural home within the flexible, non-standardized supper club format.

Digital reservation systems changed how these dinners are booked

Where earlier supper clubs often relied on word of mouth or membership, modern booking systems have made this format more broadly accessible, letting a wider range of guests discover and reserve a seat at a specific evening's table.

The format persists because it solves a specific, real desire

Despite changing considerably in specific execution across different eras, the underlying appeal — a single, shared, unpredictable evening rather than a menu of individual choices — has remained consistent, suggesting the format meets a genuine, ongoing desire rather than simply following a passing trend.

The takeaway

Today's chef-led supper club format draws on a long history of informal home hospitality, speakeasy-era social dining, and more recent pop-up and tasting-menu traditions. Understanding that history adds context to what might otherwise seem like a purely modern dining trend.
